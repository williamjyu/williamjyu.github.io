**The Romance of Chance: Exploring Gambling's Prevalence During the French and British Enlightenments**

Gambling blossomed in 17th-century Europe with the first organized gambling houses, where people of all classes wagered their salaries, savings, and fortunes.

The lottery, stocks, and the growing flow of money, pillars of the emerging capitalist system, were rooted in gambling under the guise of investing in the nation. The 1720 South Sea Company scheme drew in men, women, rich and poor alike. The new quantitative view of life, from Newton’s mathematics to Halley’s 1693 pricing of life insurance, put a number on the future. The allure of probability, formalized in money, fed the appeal of the gaming table. Writers like Richard Hey (1783) condemned gambling as corrupting and fundamentally unsocial, even as its popularity grew out of the same developments in reason and logic. Gambling’s persistence suggests an economy built, in Jessica Richard’s words, “not on rational calculation but on romance,” and its toll on the working classes complicates how we read the period’s progress.
